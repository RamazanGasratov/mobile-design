# Avoid generic AI-generated mobile design

Use this reference when creating a screen, defining a visual system, or revising work described as generic, templated, over-polished, or “AI-looking.”

## 1. Why generic results happen

Generic design begins with decoration before product understanding. Models reach for high-salience patterns — glass cards, gradient blobs, giant titles, floating pills, uniform rounded rectangles, metric grids — because they are easy to compose, not because the task needs them.

The remedy is not another visual preset. It is a specific hierarchy derived from real content and platform behavior.

Apple's 2026 branding guidance reinforces this: preserve familiar platform UI for navigation and actions, then express identity through the content layer.

## 2. Start with evidence

Determine:

- who opens the screen, where, and for how long;
- what they must notice, decide, or do first;
- which real data shapes dominate: prose, imagery, maps, timelines, conversations, media, forms, or instruments;
- whether the experience should feel calm, urgent, playful, precise, intimate, social, or operational;
- which existing brand and product cues deserve continuity;
- which platform patterns already solve the interaction.

Use real or structurally realistic content. Placeholder copy and identical cards hide hierarchy problems.

## 3. Form a design thesis

Choose one idea rooted in the product, then translate it into:

- information density;
- dominant content form;
- typography character and scale range;
- color behavior;
- shape language;
- image treatment;
- motion character.

“Glassmorphism,” “minimal,” “modern,” and “premium” are not product theses. They describe styling without intent.

## 4. Compose from the primary task

- Give the primary content most of the area and strongest hierarchy.
- Put frequent actions close to what they affect.
- Use progressive disclosure for secondary detail.
- Let irregular real content create meaningful rhythm; do not force every item into equal cards.
- Use whitespace to group and pace, not to make a sparse screen look expensive.
- Create one clear focal relationship rather than several equally loud sections.
- Keep native navigation familiar; place distinctive brand expression in content, words, imagery, data visualization, color, and purposeful motion.

## 5. Common template tells

These are warnings, not universal bans. Keep one only when product logic justifies it.

- a greeting and oversized generic headline above every home screen;
- interchangeable metric cards regardless of domain;
- purple-blue gradients, glowing orbs, and noise behind unrelated products;
- every section enclosed in a rounded card;
- the same radius on chips, fields, sheets, images, and large panels;
- an unexplained floating pill at the bottom;
- icon-only actions using arbitrary symbols;
- excessive centered alignment in task-oriented screens;
- marketing-scale typography in a frequently used utility;
- decorative charts with no decision attached;
- gradients compensating for weak hierarchy;
- glass throughout the content layer;
- invented testimonials, avatars, streaks, badges, or sparkle symbols;
- copy like “Unlock your potential” that could belong to any product.

## 6. Make the product recognizable

Prefer distinctiveness from:

- how the product's real content is visualized;
- a task-specific interaction or transition;
- a deliberate and accessible type hierarchy;
- restrained, recognizable color behavior;
- domain-relevant iconography or imagery;
- layout rhythm shaped by use frequency and content importance;
- details that reward repeated use rather than screenshot novelty.

Custom components require refinement. Spend that effort on the areas with the greatest product impact, and use standard components for utilitarian behavior.

## 7. Design review questions

1. If the logo, title, and accent color changed, could this become an unrelated app? If yes, make content expression and hierarchy more domain-specific.
2. If blur, gradient, shadow, and mockup imagery disappeared, would the layout remain clear and distinctive? If no, repair structure first.
3. Does every rounded container represent a real group or interaction? If no, remove containers.
4. Is the most visually prominent thing also the most important thing? If no, rebalance.
5. Did custom styling improve the task or merely depart from the platform? Revert unjustified novelty.
6. Can the design accommodate empty, error, long-content, localization, and accessibility states? If no, the concept is only a poster.

## 8. Review language

Name the cause and consequence.

- Weak: “This looks generic.”
- Useful: “All sections use identical cards and visual weight, so the live recording — the primary task — does not dominate.”

Recommend the smallest structural change that restores product specificity before suggesting more decoration.
